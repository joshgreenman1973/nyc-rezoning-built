What rezoning built

In the last quarter century New York City has run two opposite rezoning programs. From 2002 to 2013 it redrew whole neighborhoods, and mostly downward: - acres closed to density, by the city's own classification, against - opened, largely along corridors and downtowns. Since 2016 it has worked site by site in the other direction: - rezonings letting housing in, most smaller than two acres, and none its records mark as closing anything. Each program did what it was built to do. This page maps all - rezonings since - and counts the homes finished inside every line.

What happened inside the lines

Net new permanent homes completed inside each group of boundaries, per acre, counting only completions after the rezoning took effect. Read this as two separate stories rather than one ratio: the downzoned neighborhoods stayed roughly as they were, which is what those rezonings were for, and the upzoned sites filled in with housing, which is what those were for. Each program, by this measure, worked.

Why this comparison is hard

These two groups are not the same kind of object, and the difference flatters the upzonings before a single building is counted.

A rezoning that allowed more housing has a median footprint of - acres. It is usually drawn tightly around a single development site, often one that is already designed and financed, and frequently applied for because that project exists. A downzoning has a median footprint of - acres and encloses a whole existing neighborhood: streets, parks and thousands of houses that were already built and were never going to be replaced.

So "homes per acre" is partly a measure of how tightly the lines were drawn. Take the extreme case: a one acre rezoning for a 300 unit tower scores 300 per acre, while a thousand acre downzoning of a neighborhood that stays exactly as it was scores zero. That produces an enormous ratio and tells you nothing about what zoning did.

The direction of the finding survives every test below. The magnitude does not, and no single number should be quoted as the answer.

What this does not prove

read before quoting any number here

This is a record of what happened inside the lines, not a measurement of what the rezoning caused. A boundary that contains four thousand new homes may have been the reason they were built, or it may have been drawn around land that was going to be developed regardless. Nothing here separates the two. Read every figure as "what got built here", never as "what this rezoning produced".

  • The housing data starts in 2010. A rezoning adopted in 2004 cannot have its first six years of construction counted. Rates on this page are divided by the years actually observed, not by years since adoption, so cohorts are comparable, but the totals for older rezonings are still floors rather than full counts.
  • Most rezonings are unclassified. The city publishes a purpose classification only for the 2002 to 2013 program, and Mandatory Inclusionary Housing identifies upzonings from 2016 on. Everything else, - of the mature rezonings here, carries no label and is not counted in the comparison between raising and lowering density.
  • Boundaries overlap. About 15 percent of rezoned land has been rezoned more than once. A building inside two boundaries counts toward both at the rezoning level, and the group totals in the bars and squares above are sums over overlapping shapes. Citywide figures deduplicate by job, so they do not equal the sum of the rows. The effect is small for the two classified groups and large for the unclassified pile, where deduplicating cuts the total from about 192,000 homes to about 141,000.
  • Some of the map is missing. The source layer holds - adopted amendments; - of them carry no effective date, all from 1977 to 1994, and are excluded because there is no way to say which completions came after them. One boundary in the layer is also a fragment: the Brookville rezoning is drawn at 8 acres where the city's own table says 295.
  • The rates shown are per acre, not per acre per year. The observed-years correction is used to decide which rezonings are old enough to judge, not to scale the bars. Dividing by observed years widens the gap rather than narrowing it, so the bars are the more conservative presentation.
  • "Homes" means net permanent units. Demolitions and conversions subtract. A rezoning can therefore post a negative number, and some do.
  • A rezoning is not the only thing on the land. Tax abatements, interest rates, land assembly, the state's affordable housing programs and the general economy all moved during these decades, and none of them are held constant here.
  • Lowering density is not automatically a failure. Contextual rezonings were adopted for stated reasons, including preserving existing built form. Judging them only by homes produced measures them against a goal many of them did not have.

How this was built

Four public datasets, joined in space. Full detail in the methodology.

  • Boundaries are the Department of City Planning's adopted zoning map amendments, projected to New York State Plane so that acreage is measured rather than estimated.
  • Housing is the department's own housing database: every residential job completed since 2010, geocoded, measured as net change in permanent units. Each is dropped into whichever boundaries contain it.
  • Purpose is the city's own where the city states one: its published classification of the 2002 to 2013 program, joined on ULURP number. For 2016 onward the city publishes no purpose field, so upzonings are identified by the presence of a Mandatory Inclusionary Housing area, which requires affordable housing in exchange for added capacity. That identification is this project's inference, not the city's label, and it produces most of the upzonings counted here.

Everything substantive here (the concept, the choice of data, the questions asked of it and the desired layout) was directed by the author, a human. The coding, the spatial work and the file wrangling were primarily in the hands of Anthropic's Claude and Claude Code, under close and skeptical direction.

Quality control actually performed on this project: acreage is computed in a projected coordinate system rather than from degrees; an early version wrongly attributed Mandatory Inclusionary Housing areas to older rezonings that merely contained them, which was caught and fixed by requiring the dates to match; rates are divided by observed years so that rezonings predating the housing data are not flattered or penalised; citywide totals are deduplicated by job number because boundaries overlap; the purpose classification is the city's own, with no category invented here; the fetch scripts fail loudly rather than write a truncated file; and every headline figure was independently recomputed from the raw data by a reviewer working blind, with no knowledge of how the page was built.

The risks of using AI remain. If you spot a red flag in anything presented here, please say so.
